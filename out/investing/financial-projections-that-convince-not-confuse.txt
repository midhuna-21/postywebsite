12:T7e2,Financial projections play a significant role in any pitch to an investor, but they can feel daunting for first-time founders. Your objective is not to deliver spot-on predictions, but rather to tell a plausible story about how your business can grow. Start with sensible assumptions. Don't make wild guesses about market size, conversion rates, or pricing! Your numbers should be based on research, similar companies, or early customer activity. Informing investors of your assumptions will not only show integrity, it will build trust. Be simple. Most investors recognize that three to five year projections are unrealistic, and simply want to see where future revenues come from. A simple summary of revenues, expenses, and key metrics like customer acquisition cost and gross margin is all most investors want to see—the less detail, the better. Clarity is much more convincing than complexity. Show different scenarios. Generally, it is useful to show a base case, a conservative case, and a best-case. This process demonstrates you have considered risk and the way in which your business will respond to different market scenarios.Clearly explain your numbers. When making a pitch to investors, walk them through the logic of your projections and be ready to answer questions about how you derived each number out. Understand, not memorize, is the basis for confidence. Finally, use projections to tell a story and not just present a bunch of numbers; numbers should convey the incredible potential of your business and the real impact of important decisions. Financial projections, when done effectively, allow the discussion to shift from uncertainty to opportunity. In the words of one veteran VC:  A solid projection is not about being right; it’s about demonstrating an understanding of your business and having a plan for growing it. If founders can do that, we listen. By carefully considering financials, founders can better use the numbers as persuasive tools rather than confuse or deter
