National Park Service Workers Flag Materials as Disparaging to U.S. History

  • by Daniel Ruiz
  • 10th September 2025

National Park Service workers tagged content in the federal parks that reference slavery, Native Americans' displacement from their land, and the climate that they thought might be disparaging to Americans, based on the directive from the Trump administration.

The directive, stemming from an executive order issued in March, requires the Department of the Interior and all affiliated agencies to review materials, signage, exhibits, and other displays on public property to ensure that nothing contained within them could be interpreted as disparaging to Americans, either past or present. The overarching goal of this initiative, as stated in the Interior Department's press release, is to prioritize content that emphasizes the achievements, accomplishments, and progress of the American people, while highlighting the beauty, abundance, and grandeur of the nation’s landscapes. This review has been framed as a way to celebrate American history in a manner that is uplifting, educational, and inspirational. National Park Service employees and other relevant personnel were directed to identify all potentially disparaging materials by July 18, 2025, and to remove, cover, or otherwise adjust them by September 17, 2025. Among the examples of materials flagged for review were exhibits dealing with slavery and racial brutality in states such as South Carolina and Pennsylvania, accounts and displays covering Native American history and forced assimilation in Florida and Alaska, and interpretive signage discussing pollution, environmental degradation, and climate change impacts in states such as North Carolina. Supporters of the initiative argue that this approach will foster a more unified national narrative and present Americans with a more positive and celebratory understanding of the country’s accomplishments. They contend that focusing on stories of innovation, resilience, and achievement can provide educational value while boosting civic pride and national cohesion. Critics, however, have raised significant concerns about the implications of such a review process. Many historians, educators, and advocacy groups argue that removing or suppressing discussions of slavery, racial injustice, Native American displacement, and environmental harm could amount to historical whitewashing. They warn that this approach risks erasing the struggles, oppression, and systemic injustices that shaped American history and remain critical to understanding the nation’s development and contemporary challenges. These critics emphasize that omitting difficult or uncomfortable historical topics from public displays undermines public education, limits opportunities for meaningful dialogue, and may hinder reconciliation efforts among historically marginalized communities. Some argue that understanding the darker chapters of American history is essential to preventing repetition of past mistakes and fostering a well-rounded civic consciousness.

Interior Department officials, however, maintain that the directive is not intended to erase history entirely, but to reframe how it is presented in publicly accessible spaces. The initiative emphasizes context, interpretation, and balance, aiming to ensure that narratives inspire and educate without casting current Americans or the nation as a whole in a negative light. They have cited examples of ways the directive could be implemented, such as providing additional interpretive signage that celebrates innovation, highlighting the contributions of underrepresented communities, and providing immersive experiences that emphasize national achievements in arts, science, industry, and environmental stewardship. National Park Service employees have been tasked with reviewing thousands of exhibits, signs, and displays across hundreds of sites nationwide, a process that requires careful coordination, documentation, and consultation with historians, cultural experts, and community stakeholders. In addition to the internal review, the Department of the Interior has announced plans to provide guidance and training for staff to help them assess materials in line with the executive order. The guidelines include criteria for determining whether an exhibit or display could be considered disparaging, recommendations for ways to reframe content to focus on achievement and positive contributions, and methods for maintaining historical accuracy while emphasizing celebratory narratives. As the review progressed, public reaction has been mixed. Many local communities and park visitors have expressed curiosity and interest in how the changes will be implemented, while others have voiced concern that the initiative could prioritize symbolism over substantive educational content. Advocacy organizations representing racial and indigenous communities have urged transparency and the inclusion of diverse perspectives in the review process to ensure that the directive does not inadvertently marginalize the experiences and histories of those groups. Several educational institutions and historians have called for the development of supplementary materials, such as brochures, online resources, and interactive exhibits, that contextualize historical injustices within a broader narrative of national achievement. They emphasize that this approach can allow visitors to engage with both challenging and inspiring aspects of American history, promoting critical thinking and nuanced understanding. The National Park Service has stated that public input will be considered throughout the implementation of the directive.

They have scheduled forums, surveys, and public comment opportunities to gather feedback from local communities, historians, educators, and other stakeholders. The agency stressed that the goal is to balance the executive order's directive to present uplifting content with the responsibility to provide historically accurate and comprehensive educational experiences. Some critics have expressed concern that, despite assurances, the process may result in selective interpretation or the omission of important historical events. They argue that exhibits highlighting systemic oppression, slavery, forced displacement, and environmental harm are crucial for fostering empathy, accountability, and a deeper understanding of the complex forces that have shaped American society. Proponents counter that the initiative does not call for the deletion of all references to historical injustices but instead encourages curators and staff to present these topics in a manner that also highlights resilience, progress, and positive contributions made by Americans throughout history. Implementation of the directive also involves logistical challenges. Many exhibits are permanent or semi-permanent and require physical modifications, additional materials, or interpretive signage to comply with the directive. The Department of the Interior has allocated resources to support park sites in this process, including funding for updated educational materials, temporary covers for flagged content, and the hiring of additional staff to oversee compliance. Park managers have emphasized the importance of carefully documenting changes to ensure historical integrity and to provide a record for future review or potential reversal of decisions. In states like South Carolina, Pennsylvania, Florida, Alaska, and North Carolina, where exhibits have been specifically flagged, the process involves close collaboration with local historical societies, indigenous groups, civil rights organizations, and environmental experts. These consultations aim to ensure that the revised displays reflect diverse perspectives while adhering to the overarching goal of highlighting achievement, progress, and national pride. The directive has also generated broader public discourse on the role of national parks, museums, and publicly funded spaces in shaping collective memory.

Experts in museum studies and public history note that these spaces serve not only as educational sites but also as tools for civic engagement, identity formation, and cultural diplomacy. Decisions about which narratives to emphasize can influence visitors' understanding of the past, perceptions of contemporary social issues, and attitudes toward civic participation. Some educational advocates have proposed that the directive could be an opportunity to develop innovative interpretive strategies, such as interactive exhibits, augmented reality experiences, and multimedia storytelling, that convey both the challenges and accomplishments of American history in a compelling and accessible manner. They argue that thoughtful integration of technology and narrative framing can create a richer educational experience while remaining faithful to historical accuracy. The National Park Service has reiterated that the directive’s purpose is to provide a framework for review rather than to dictate specific content. Curators, park managers, and educators are encouraged to exercise professional judgment, collaborate with local experts, and consider public feedback in determining how best to present materials. This flexible approach is intended to preserve educational integrity while complying with the executive order. In conclusion, the review directive represents a significant federal initiative to reassess the presentation of history and culture on public property. By seeking to minimize content deemed disparaging while emphasizing national achievement and landscape grandeur, the initiative has sparked widespread discussion about historical interpretation, educational value, and the role of public institutions in shaping national identity. The process involves comprehensive review, stakeholder consultation, and public engagement, with the goal of creating displays that are inspiring, educational, and reflective of the nation’s accomplishments. While debate continues regarding potential risks of whitewashing and selective interpretation, federal officials maintain that careful implementation, transparency, and community involvement can ensure that the directive supports both national pride and historical understanding, ultimately offering visitors a balanced, informative, and engaging experience of American history and culture.
